Is it the driver, or is it the car?

Formula 1 has argued about this for seventy years and never settled it. We settled it — or at least put a number on it — with one regression and twenty-four years of race results.

No F1 knowledge required  ·  10 minute read

If you've never watched a race

Sixty seconds on how the sport works

You only need five facts. Everything after this builds on them.

The teams
Ten teams. Each one designs and builds its own car from scratch. The cars are wildly different from each other — a good one can be a full lap faster over a race than a bad one.
The drivers
Each team runs exactly two drivers, so twenty cars start each race. Drivers change teams every few years.
A race weekend
Saturday is qualifying: one fast lap each, and your lap time sets your starting position. Sunday is the race, roughly two hours.
Finishing
You're scored on where you finish, 1st through 20th. Cars break down often enough to matter — about a quarter of entries don't finish cleanly.
Teammates
The two drivers in a team get identical cars. This turns out to be the single most useful fact in the entire project.
Why anyone argues
Lewis Hamilton has seven championships. Fernando Alonso has two. Hamilton also spent eight years in the fastest car on the grid. So how much of that gap is him?

The problem

You never see a driver without a car

Every result in the sport's history is a driver and a machine tangled together. There is no race where we observe driving alone.

This is the statistical problem at the centre of the project, and it isn't unique to racing. It's the same shape as asking whether a fund manager is skilled or just working at a firm with good research, or whether a surgeon has better outcomes or just healthier patients. Two causes, one observed result, and no experiment available.

You can't fix it by looking harder at the leaderboard. Hamilton's seven titles are perfectly consistent with him being the greatest driver alive and with him being ordinary in a rocket ship. The data as it sits cannot tell those apart.

The way in

Drivers change teams. That's the whole trick.

Here's the move. If results were mostly about the driver, changing cars shouldn't matter much. If results were mostly about the car, a driver's results should lurch the moment he switches teams — even though he's the same person who was fast last November.

Daniel Ricciardo averaged a 4th place finish at Red Bull in 2018. He moved to Renault. In 2019 he averaged 9th. He did not forget how to drive over one winter.

Every one of those moves is a comparison the data hands us for free. And they chain together: Ricciardo links Red Bull to Renault, someone else links Renault to Alpine, and so on across two decades until every car on the grid is connected to every other one through some driver who drove both. That chain is what lets us eventually say something about a driver in a car he never actually sat in.

Look at Ricciardo, top right. The line falls off a cliff in 2019 and again in 2023. Look at Alonso, bottom left: Ferrari in 2014, then McLaren in 2015, and he drops seven places overnight. Nothing about the driver changed. The car did.

Economists call this design a two-way fixed effects model, and use it to separate how much of your salary is you versus where you work — identified off people who change jobs. Same math, better livery.

The model

Two lines of code

We assume a race result is a few things added together:

where you finish  =  baseline
   + how good the driver is
   + how good the car is
   + race-day chaos

We know the finishing position, and we know which driver and which car produced it. The regression solves for the two middle terms: one number per driver, one number per car, chosen so that adding the right pair together comes as close as possible to reproducing 7,000 real race results.

Two details worth knowing, because they're the only judgement calls that really matter:

That chart is why the second decision below matters. Starting position tracks finishing position almost one-for-one at the front — and qualifying is itself a driving task. Control for it and you have quietly deleted most of the driver's contribution before you ever measure it.

  • We threw out breakdowns. A driver whose engine dies on lap 3 still gets recorded in some position. That's the team's reliability, not his driving, so those races are excluded.
  • We ignored where drivers started. This feels wrong at first — starting position is obviously the best predictor of finishing position. But qualifying is driving. Controlling for it would quietly hide the very thing we're trying to measure. Leaving it out is what makes the whole analysis work.

Play with it long enough and the finding becomes obvious: swapping the car moves the answer far more than swapping the driver does. Put the best driver on the grid in a midfield car and he finishes tenth. Put a mediocre driver in a Ferrari and he's still near the front. That's the whole paper in one control panel.

Is any of this real?

We checked it a completely different way

Everything above comes out of one model, which means it could be an artifact of our modelling choices. So we tested it against a measure that uses no model at all.

Remember fact five from the primer: teammates drive identical cars. So we can just count. Across every race, how often did each driver finish ahead of the guy in the other identical car? No regression, no assumptions, just a tally.

Agreement between the two methods

r = −0.65

Negative only because a lower driver effect means a better driver. Drivers our model rates highly are the same drivers who beat their teammates — two independent measures, same answer.

It isn't a perfect match, and it shouldn't be. Beating your teammate depends entirely on which teammate you happened to get — being paired with a rookie flatters you. The model draws on the whole network at once instead, which is precisely why we trust it more.

Where we'd push back on ourselves

Four things this doesn't prove

  • We assumed skill just adds on. A driver's number is treated as something he carries into any car unchanged. Real racing isn't that clean — a slow car gives you fewer chances to actually use your pace, and some cars suit some driving styles.
  • Drivers aren't assigned to cars randomly. Good teams hire good drivers. That's the 12% overlap, and no amount of statistics fully untangles it.
  • We deleted the breakdowns. Right call for measuring driving, but it means a fast driver in an unreliable car looks better here than his actual season did.
  • These are career averages. Twenty-four years compressed into one number per driver. It says nothing about who was quick in any particular year.

The short version

If someone asks you what we did

We took 7,000 races, fit one number per driver and one per car, and used drivers who switched teams to tell the two apart.

The car explains about 42% of where you finish. The driver explains about 7%. The best driver in a midfield car finishes around tenth, and no amount of talent gets him a win.

In Formula 1, the machinery decides the range of results available to you. The driver decides where inside that range you land.
